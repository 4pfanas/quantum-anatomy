What is a quantum computer?

A quantum computer is a machine that computes with the rules of quantum mechanics instead of switching bits on and off. It is built for problems where the number of possibilities explodes, like simulating molecules, and it is still a young technology. Below: the ideas, the hardware drawn as two blueprints you can pull apart, and where it is all heading.

It is a problem-solving machine like any computer, but its answers often come as probabilities rather than one certain value, and the sensible plan is to split work between quantum and classical hardware.

Four ideas that make it tick

Qubits only make sense through quantum mechanics. Four principles cover most of what you need.

01Superposition

A qubit can hold a blend of many possible states at once. Measure it and the blend collapses to a plain 0 or 1.

02Entanglement

Qubits can be linked so their results are correlated. Reading one tells you something about the others.

03Interference

Each outcome has a wave-like amplitude. Waves can add up or cancel, and the whole trick is steering the right answers to add and the wrong ones to cancel.

04Decoherence

A qubit loses its quantum behaviour when the world pokes it, by heat, noise or being measured. Most of the hardware exists to delay this.

Put together, a program prepares a superposition, lets entanglement and interference shape it under an algorithm, then amplifies the right outcomes and cancels the others. What is left, once you measure, is the answer.

How do quantum computers work?

The one big difference from a laptop is the unit of information. A bit is 0 or 1. A qubit is 0, 1, or a weighted mix of both, and every extra qubit doubles the number of combinations the machine can describe.

Why does that help?

A quantum computer does not simply try every path at once. Think of a maze: it works with amplitudes for each route, lets them overlap, and arranges for the dead ends to cancel out so that the routes that work stand out when you measure.

Quantum computing components

A whole system is roughly the size of a car, so these machines are not portable. Most people use them over the cloud. The two blueprints below take the hardware apart; here is the vocabulary first.

Quantum processor

The chip holds the physical qubits and the structures that keep them in place. Add the control electronics and the classical hardware around it and you have a quantum processing unit (QPU). See Fig 2.

Superconductors

Chilled enough, certain metals let electrons pair up and tunnel through thin insulators. That forms Josephson junctions, the key part of a superconducting qubit. The fridge in Fig 1 does the chilling.

Control

These qubits are small circuits of capacitors and Josephson junctions. Microwave pulses change their state and read it back out.

Software

Hardware alone is not enough. A stable software stack is needed too, and open-source toolkits such as IBM's Qiskit sit between programmers and the chip.

    Classical versus quantum

    Both kinds of computer sit on quantum physics, but a classical one does not use quantum effects for computation. Classical machines run bits through logic step by step, from a phone to a supercomputer. A quantum computer runs qubits using superposition and interference.

    ClassicalQuantum
    Unit of dataBit: 0 or 1Qubit: 0, 1 or a superposition
    Where you find itNearly every deviceSpecialised, mostly cloud-accessed hardware
    How it solvesLogic on bits, in sequenceInterference steers amplitudes toward answers
    Typical answerOne exact resultOften a probability range

    When is quantum better?

    Classical computers stay the right tool for most jobs. Quantum earns its place on problems with many interacting variables, such as the behaviour of molecules. Classical supercomputers brute-force those and stall on all but simple ones, while a quantum machine can mimic the molecular system directly.

    What it could be used for

    The idea dates to the early 1980s. In 1994 Peter Shor published a factoring algorithm that could in principle break strong cryptography, which put quantum computing on security teams' radar. Engineering firms, banks and shipping companies are now testing uses.

    Pharmaceuticals

    Simulate molecules to speed up the search for new drugs and treatments.

    Chemistry

    Look for better catalysts, petrochemical alternatives and carbon breakdown processes that cut emissions.

    Machine learning

    AI is straining hardware and energy budgets. Quantum algorithms may offer new ways to analyse data and speed up some problems.

    IBM's overview says IBM, Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Rigetti and IonQ are all investing, and cites an estimate of a USD 1.3 trillion industry by 2035. Treat forecasts like that as estimates.

    Utility, advantage and the road ahead

    Quantum computing is still being developed. Two milestones mark the way, and they are easy to mix up.

    NOWQuantum utility

    Reliable, accurate results on problems too big for brute-force classical simulation, where before only specialised approximations worked. IBM first showed this in 2023.

    NEXTQuantum advantage

    A quantum method beats every known classical one on speed, quality or cost, with a real practical benefit. IBM said it expected the first cases by the end of 2026, if the quantum and high-performance computing communities work together.

    How progress is measured

    • Layer fidelity: how well a processor runs circuits overall, with detail on individual qubits, gates and crosstalk.
    • Processing speed (CLOPS): circuit layer operations per second, counting both quantum and classical parts.
    • Circuit depth: how many gate steps run before the qubits decohere. Deeper means more complex programs.

    Six challenges

    1. Scaling processors. IBM's stated plan is 200 logical qubits running 100 million gates by 2029, and 2,000 logical qubits running a billion gates by 2033.
    2. Scaling hardware. Qubits are error-prone and need extreme cooling, so teams work on smaller, cheaper, lower-energy systems.
    3. Error correction. Spread information over more qubits so mistakes can be caught. IBM announced a code in 2024 it called about ten times more efficient than earlier ones.
    4. Finding algorithms. Advantage needs circuits shown to beat classical methods, and discovering them is what moves the field from utility to advantage.
    5. Software and middleware. A stable, fast software stack. Qiskit is the most widely used quantum toolkit and also works with other hardware types.
    6. Quantum-centric supercomputing. Quantum processors working alongside classical supercomputers, each doing the part it is good at.

    The numbers behind the drawings

    Control lines lose most of their power on the way down on purpose. Warm parts of the cable radiate microwave noise, and attenuators at each colder stage soak it up so the qubits only hear the pulse.

    StageTypical temperatureControl line lossWhat sits here

    Total attenuation from room temperature to the chip is about 56 dB in this wiring, so a 1 mW pulse arrives near 2.5 nW. Labs differ on exact values and placement (for example, the quantum-limited amplifier is placed at the cold plate in some setups and the mixing chamber in others), so read the drawing as a typical layout, not a specific machine.

    Why so cold?

    • A 5 GHz microwave photon carries energy equal to about 0.24 K of heat. At room temperature the surroundings are full of such photons and would scramble the qubit instantly.
    • At 15 mK the chance that thermal energy excites a 5 GHz qubit is about e−0.24/0.015 ≈ 10−7. The qubit sits in its ground state until a pulse says otherwise.
    • Aluminium and niobium become superconducting at these temperatures, which is what lets a loop of current act as a qubit with almost no loss.

    What the drawings simplify

    • Real fridges have more plates, many more cables (hundreds to thousands per machine at scale), and extra filtering and thermal anchoring that is left out here.
    • Fig 2's qubit demo is a classical stand-in for ideal gates on a handful of qubits. It follows the rules (superposition collapses on measurement, an entangled pair gives matching or opposite results) but does not simulate noise.
